Ginkgo Biloba: What It Is, Evidence, Dose & Side Effects

By the ZetaStore editorial team · Last checked November 9, 2026 · Summary of published research, not medical advice

Ingredient guide · Independent summary

Ginkgo biloba is a tree native to China; its leaf extract is sold for memory, circulation, and other wellness claims.

Quick answer

What it is: An herbal supplement most often made from ginkgo leaves.

Commonly used for: Memory, cognitive health, and circulation, among other advertised uses.

Evidence: Research does not show that it prevents dementia; possible symptom benefits in diagnosed dementia remain uncertain.

Typical study doses: Trials have used standardized extracts, including 120 mg twice daily in a large prevention trial; that is a study description, not a dosing recommendation.

Main cautions: Bleeding risk and possible medication interactions; seeds and crude plant material can be toxic.

Ask a clinician first: Especially if pregnant, taking medicines, managing diabetes, prone to seizures, or planning surgery.

What is Ginkgo Biloba?

Ginkgo biloba, often called ginkgo or maidenhair tree, is a long-lived tree species. Supplements are usually made from its leaves. The seed and crude plant are different from the processed leaf extracts used in many clinical studies, and they should not be treated as interchangeable.

Common forms include tablets, capsules, liquid extracts, and teas. Research on supplements usually concerns a specific extract rather than any product containing the word “ginkgo.” Some extracts are standardized, meaning the manufacturer specifies amounts or proportions of selected plant constituents. A standardized label does not by itself establish that a supplement has the same composition as a studied extract or will produce the same effects.

The U.S. National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) notes that ginkgo leaf extract is the form most commonly used today. Ginkgo is promoted for a range of health goals, but research on one extract or use cannot automatically be applied to another product or condition.

What Ginkgo Biloba is used for

Vendor claims: Supplement marketing commonly presents ginkgo as support for memory, focus, “brain health,” or circulation. Some products also make claims related to tinnitus, anxiety, or other wellness outcomes. These are claims about intended use, not proof that a product works.

People may take ginkgo because they are concerned about age-related memory changes, have a diagnosis affecting cognition, or are seeking general cognitive support. Others may use it for perceived circulation benefits. These motivations differ from the question of whether clinical trials have demonstrated a meaningful benefit.

What the research says about Ginkgo Biloba

Dementia prevention and cognitive decline

A large randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial followed 3,069 adults aged 75 or older for a median of 6.1 years. Participants received 120 mg of ginkgo extract twice daily or placebo; the trial found no reduction in the incidence of dementia or Alzheimer’s disease with ginkgo. This is direct evidence against using that studied regimen to prevent dementia in a similar population, not proof about every possible extract or outcome. PubMed: Ginkgo Evaluation of Memory trial

NCCIH also summarizes three large studies in older adults with normal cognition or mild cognitive impairment that did not find reduced dementia risk. Its overall assessment is that there is no conclusive evidence ginkgo helps a health condition, and the effect on cognitive performance in healthy people remains uncertain. NCCIH: Ginkgo

Symptoms in people diagnosed with dementia

A Cochrane systematic review included 82 randomized studies with 10,613 participants overall, although only 72 studies with 9,783 participants contributed extractable data. For people with dementia, pooled six-month findings suggested possible improvements in overall status, cognition, and daily activities, but certainty was low and results varied considerably between studies. The review notes that many studies had methodological problems and few tested treatment beyond six months. Cochrane: Ginkgo for cognitive impairment and dementia

Mild cognitive impairment and healthy adults

In the same Cochrane review, evidence from trials in mild cognitive impairment indicated little or no difference from placebo in overall condition, cognitive scores, or everyday activities at six months. For healthy adults, NCCIH describes much of the research as low quality and says it is uncertain whether supplements influence cognitive performance. Evidence therefore does not support treating ginkgo as established memory enhancement.

Circulation, tinnitus, and other uses

Ginkgo is also marketed for circulation and tinnitus. NCCIH reports that research suggests ginkgo is not helpful for relieving tinnitus or treating high blood pressure, and does not support its use to prevent heart disease. For several other proposed uses, a small amount of evidence exists, but the overall findings are inconclusive. NCCIH: evidence by condition

Ginkgo Biloba dosage: what studies used

Doses depend on the extract, the condition being studied, and the trial design. In the GEM prevention trial, researchers used 120 mg of extract twice daily and followed participants for a median of 6.1 years; that regimen did not prevent dementia. The Cochrane review examined randomized trials of different durations and populations, including six-month comparisons for mild cognitive impairment and dementia. Those study schedules describe research, not an amount to take.

There is no single study dose that can be applied to all commercial ginkgo products. Extract concentration, standardization, and serving definitions vary. NCCIH and MedlinePlus do not establish a universal consumer upper limit in the materials reviewed here. Do not use a trial dose as personal guidance; discuss a product and its label with a healthcare professional.

Side effects, interactions and who should avoid Ginkgo Biloba

Reported side effects of leaf extracts include headache, dizziness, and gastrointestinal symptoms. Raw or roasted ginkgo seeds and crude plant material can cause serious adverse effects; seeds are not a substitute for leaf extract. NCCIH safety information

Ginkgo may increase bleeding risk when combined with anticoagulant or antiplatelet medicines. Mayo Clinic also flags possible interactions with ibuprofen, some seizure medicines, certain antidepressants, some statins, and diabetes medicines. These examples are not a complete interaction list. Ask a doctor or pharmacist to check your specific medicines and supplements rather than relying on a general list. Mayo Clinic: Ginkgo interactions and safety

People who are pregnant should avoid self-starting ginkgo: NCCIH says it may be unsafe during pregnancy and could contribute to early labor or additional bleeding near delivery. Safety during breastfeeding is not well known. Mayo Clinic advises discussing use before surgery and notes a two-week precaution; follow the surgical team’s instructions, since stopping or continuing supplements should be coordinated with them.

People with diabetes should discuss ginkgo with their healthcare professional because it may affect blood sugar management or the response to diabetes medicines. People with seizure disorders should also seek advice: Mayo Clinic cautions that large amounts may increase seizure risk. Older adults and anyone with a bleeding condition or taking prescription or over-the-counter medicines should check before use.

How to read a Ginkgo Biloba label

Check whether the product uses leaf extract, and look for the amount per serving and the number of capsules or tablets that make up a serving. If the label says “standardized,” note what constituents or percentages it lists. A standardized product is not automatically equivalent to the extract used in a named trial.

Third-party quality seals, such as USP or NSF, can indicate that a product was tested for aspects of identity, contents, manufacturing, or contaminants. They do not prove clinical effectiveness or guarantee safety for a particular person. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements explains what quality seals can and cannot tell consumers. NIH ODS: Dietary Supplements—What You Need to Know

Be cautious with proprietary blends that hide the amount of each ingredient, vague claims such as “clinically proven,” or labels that imply a supplement can prevent or treat a disease. A marketing phrase is not a substitute for evidence from relevant human trials.

Ginkgo Biloba FAQ

Is Ginkgo Biloba safe to take every day?

Some adults have used leaf extracts in research, but personal safety depends on health conditions, medicines, product, and duration. Ginkgo can cause side effects and interactions; ask a healthcare professional before regular use.

How long does Ginkgo Biloba take to work?

There is no dependable timeline established for general memory or wellness claims. Studies have used different durations and results vary by condition; possible dementia symptom findings in some trials do not establish a reliable time to benefit.

Does Ginkgo Biloba interact with medications?

Yes. It may increase bleeding risk with blood thinners or antiplatelet medicines and may interact with other drugs, including some diabetes and seizure medicines. Ask a pharmacist or clinician to review your medication list.

Can Ginkgo Biloba prevent dementia?

A large randomized trial did not find that ginkgo reduced dementia incidence, and NCCIH says it has not been shown to prevent or slow dementia progression.

Are ginkgo seeds the same as ginkgo leaf extract?

No. Most supplements and clinical studies concern leaf extracts. Raw or roasted seeds and crude plant material can be toxic and should not be substituted for studied extracts.
