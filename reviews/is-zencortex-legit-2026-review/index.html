ZenCortex ingredients and what is included

ZenCortex is presented as liquid drops rather than capsules. The vendor says that each drop formula contains more than 20 selected ingredients and names eight examples: grape seed, green tea, Gymnema sylvestre, Capsicum annuum, Panax ginseng, astragalus, chromium picolinate, and maca root. Its own page associates these ingredients with hearing, circulation, inflammatory-response, or energy-support language. Those are vendor descriptions, not proof that the finished product produces those effects.

The page does not name the other ingredients in its “over 20” blend or state serving size, servings per bottle, or amounts per serving. The supplied 300-pixel product image shows bottles but no readable Supplement Facts panel. Request the current full label before buying, particularly to check doses, allergies, or interactions.

The three- and six-bottle bundles list two downloadable e-books, “The Ultimate Tea Remedies” and “Powerful Ways to Sharpen Your Memory.” The two-bottle package lists no bonuses; the page gives little detail about the e-books.

How ZenCortex is intended to work

The vendor says the formula supports hearing, auditory health, and clear sounds, associating green tea with blood flow, Capsicum with inflammatory response, ginseng with “neuromaintaining” properties, and maca with energy. It does not explain the blend’s mechanism or provide tested doses.

Customer stories on the sales page are promotional, not controlled clinical evidence; we have not verified them or used them to predict outcomes. ZenCortex should not replace hearing evaluation, prescribed care, or clinical advice.

What independent sources say about the key ingredients

Panax ginseng. The U.S. National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health says a small amount of research suggests possible cognitive-function effects in some middle-aged adults, while most trials have been small and short. It does not establish that ginseng improves hearing or that it makes ZenCortex effective. NCCIH also notes potential insomnia, medication interactions, and concerns for some people with autoimmune conditions, blood-clotting issues, diabetes, pregnancy, or breastfeeding. NCCIH: Asian Ginseng.

Chromium picolinate. NIH’s Office of Dietary Supplements reports that studies of chromium supplements have mixed results for several commonly promoted health outcomes. Its consumer fact sheet notes possible interactions with insulin, diabetes medicines, and levothyroxine; people with kidney or liver disease should be cautious about high amounts. The ZenCortex page supplies no chromium dose, and this general ingredient evidence is not evidence that chromium—or this formula—supports hearing. NIH ODS: Chromium Fact Sheet for Consumers.

Evidence boundary: Research on an ingredient is not proof about ZenCortex as a finished product. The product’s exact ingredient amounts are not available in the reviewed materials, which prevents a dose-to-study comparison.
