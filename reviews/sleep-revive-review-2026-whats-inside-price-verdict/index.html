What Sleep Revive is and what's included

Sleep Revive is marketed as a dietary supplement for nighttime use. The vendor says each bottle has 60 vegetarian capsules and recommends two capsules 30–45 minutes before bed, or 30 nightly servings.

The official sales script names rutaecarpine, lemon balm extract, valerian root, L-theanine, and magnesium glycinate. The supplied product image and page text did not expose a readable Supplement Facts panel. Ingredient amounts, standardized extract details, and inactive ingredients therefore remain unconfirmed; request the current label if a dose or interaction matters.

How Sleep Revive is intended to work

The vendor says rutaecarpine addresses substances it calls \u201csleep disruptors,\u201d including BPA and phthalates, while the other ingredients support sleep-wake timing. It also promotes use after extra coffee. These are seller claims; the materials reviewed did not provide an independent clinical trial of the finished formula.

Ingredient research cannot establish that this particular combination works or removes BPA or phthalates. Treat the \u201c detox\u201d and circadian-reset claims as unverified, not as established medical facts.

What independent sources say about the key ingredients

Ingredient research offers context, not a product endorsement. Evidence about an ingredient does not establish Sleep Revive efficacy or its dose.

Valerian root

NCCIH says evidence for valerian and sleep is inconsistent and insufficient; long-term safety is uncertain. Discuss herbal supplements with a clinician, especially if taking medication. See the NCCIH valerian overview.

Lemon balm

A 2024 randomized crossover study tested a specific standardized lemon balm phytosome, not Sleep Revive. Some sleep measures improved, but its formulation cannot confirm effects of Sleep Revive’s unspecified extract or amount. Read the PubMed study record on Melissa officinalis phytosome.

L-theanine

A 2025 systematic review found modest improvements in some subjective sleep measures but called for more research on pure L-theanine, dose, and duration. It does not verify Sleep Revive. See the PubMed meta-analysis of L-theanine and sleep.

Magnesium

NCCIH says rigorous evidence is too limited to determine whether magnesium helps insomnia; small studies vary in quality and results. Read the NCCIH review of magnesium supplements and sleep.

FDA says it cannot approve supplements for safety and effectiveness before marketing. See FDA consumer information on dietary supplements.

Sleep Revive pros and cons

Potential advantages

  • The seller gives a clear stated routine: two capsules before bed.
  • One bottle is described as a 30-day supply, with 60 capsules.
  • The vendor names its five featured ingredients rather than presenting the product only as a proprietary "sleep blend."
  • The site lists a customer-support contact, a physical return address, and a 60-day guarantee.

Limitations to weigh

  • Ingredient amounts and a fully readable Supplement Facts label were not available in the materials reviewed.
  • We found no finished-product clinical trial in the sales materials reviewed; vendor efficacy statements are not independent proof.
  • The one-bottle price has a listed shipping charge, and the page also offers a monthly subscription.
  • The posted refund policy requires return shipping at the customer's expense and has a deadline that differs from the broad guarantee wording.

Red flags and things to verify before you buy

Subscription: the page offers a $47.20 monthly subscription, described as cancellable anytime, and a one-time order. Verify your selection and recurring charge at checkout.

Refund terms: the 60-day sales promise is qualified by a policy requiring the return to arrive within 60 days of purchase, include the original packing slip, and be shipped at the buyer's expense. Confirm the deadline and keep your order records.

Scarcity and claims: the page says a limited offer is guaranteed only for the next 100 customers; we could not verify this. Claims about BPA/phthalate removal and resetting the circadian clock lack finished-product clinical evidence in materials reviewed.
