What is Prosta Peak, and what is included?

Prosta Peak is marketed as a dietary supplement for prostate wellness. Its product image shows “60 capsules.” The sales page says to take it with water but does not state the serving size or provide a readable Supplement Facts panel with ingredient quantities.

The vendor describes a proprietary blend of “20+” plants and nutrients and names cat’s claw, green tea, pygeum, raspberry, soursop, and saw palmetto. A separate section repeats green tea extract, raspberry, saw palmetto, and pygeum as a “blend of 4 plants and minerals.” The text does not enumerate the complete formula, so this is not a full ingredient list.

Label transparency: The vendor image is a product-bottle image, not a readable Supplement Facts label. Serving size, servings per container, ingredient quantities, and the full formula remain unverified from the materials reviewed.

Advertised extras are two digital guides, “The Stronger Erection Blueprint” and “21 Day Erection Revival,” plus a VIP wellness guide/newsletter. These are not ingredients or proof of a health outcome.

How Prosta Peak is intended to work

The vendor says the blend is intended to support prostate health and urinary tract function. It describes a natural, stimulant-free formula and says to take a capsule with water. The page gives no specific mechanism, product-specific clinical study, or daily ingredient amounts.

Research on a plant ingredient cannot establish effects of Prosta Peak. Dose, extract type, manufacturing, and the combination all affect how relevant an ingredient study may be.

What independent sources say about the key ingredients

Saw palmetto. NCCIH summarizes a 2023 review of 27 studies and reports little or no benefit from saw palmetto alone for BPH symptoms; evidence for combinations remains uncertain. Prosta Peak does not disclose its dose or extract, so these findings cannot be mapped to its formula. NCCIH: Saw Palmetto.

Pygeum. A Cochrane review at PubMed found possible urinary-symptom improvements in older trials, while noting small, short studies, varied doses and preparations, and inconsistent outcome reporting. This does not prove an effect from Prosta Peak’s undisclosed dose. PubMed: Pygeum africanum for benign prostatic hyperplasia.

Green tea extract. NCCIH says evidence is inconclusive for most promoted uses. Extract supplements can cause digestive symptoms and, rarely, liver injury, and may interact with medicines. Prosta Peak provides no amount or standardization for comparison. NCCIH: Green Tea.

Ingredient research does not prove this finished product works. FDA says supplements are not approved for safety and effectiveness before marketing and advises consumers to discuss use with a health professional. FDA: Consumer Supplement Guidance.
