What is High Cholesterol? What is included?

“High Cholesterol” is the marketplace listing title. The actual offer presented on the vendor’s sales and order pages is The Oxidized Cholesterol Strategy, published by Blue Heron Health News. It is a digital guide, not a capsule, liquid, or other nutritional supplement. Accordingly, there is no supplement facts panel, serving size, ingredient list, or per-bottle supply to report.

The sales page describes a four-week, week-by-week strategy. Its stated topics include foods the author associates with oxidized cholesterol, foods to include, gradual weekly steps, and tools to monitor or manage a personal strategy. The order page says the $49 purchase is a PDF/e-book with lifetime access, unlimited downloads, and free updates. It also says customers may be offered a physical printed book at checkout for an additional printing cost; that optional offer is not included in the stated digital price.

The vendor page does not identify a course-video duration, named module titles, clinical protocol, or validated monitoring device. It does not give a package tier or an itemized breakdown beyond the digital guide. The author’s personal narrative is marketing copy, not independent evidence that the program changes cholesterol levels or cardiovascular outcomes.

How the Oxidized Cholesterol Strategy is intended to work

The vendor says the guide focuses on dietary choices connected to oxidized cholesterol and introduces actions over four weeks. Its marketing frames oxidized cholesterol and arterial plaque as central targets and suggests the method may clear plaque, lower cholesterol, and help prevent heart attacks or strokes. Those are the vendor’s claims. The sales material reviewed does not provide product-specific clinical trial results, a peer-reviewed evaluation of this guide, or evidence that readers can expect those outcomes.

Use the guide, if at all, as general reading rather than a personalized treatment plan. High cholesterol can have multiple contributors, including diet, activity, smoking, genetics, age, health conditions, and medicines. No single food ingredient explains every person’s cholesterol profile.

What independent sources say about the key method

Because this is a digital diet-and-lifestyle guide rather than an ingredient-based formula, the relevant comparison is its general method—not a list of active compounds. MedlinePlus’ cholesterol diet guidance describes several dietary levers, including limiting saturated fat, choosing healthier fats, eating soluble fiber, and including fruits and vegetables. That broader advice does not establish that this product’s specific four-week plan works.

MedlinePlus’ cholesterol overview explains that cholesterol is carried in lipoproteins, and that high LDL can contribute to plaque buildup. It also notes that high cholesterol can have lifestyle, genetic, medical-condition, and medication-related causes. That context does not validate the sales page’s “one ingredient” framing.

The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute describes cholesterol care as potentially combining clinician-guided lifestyle changes and medicines. Its treatment overview says people should discuss their levels, cardiovascular risk, other conditions, and medication benefits and side effects with a healthcare provider. Evidence supporting general diet or treatment guidance is not evidence for this specific guide, its proposed mechanism, or its claimed results.
