LYMPH TONIC ingredients: what is listed?

The vendor describes LYMPH TONIC as an alcohol-free botanical liquid, supplied in a 2 fl oz (59 mL) bottle. It says a serving is two droppers and that each container has 30 servings. The sales page states that the serving contains 600 mg of a proprietary herbal blend. It does not disclose the amount of each ingredient in that blend.

Ingredients named on the page are Boswellia serrata extract, baicalein, omega-3 fatty acids, curcumin, horse chestnut extract, and nattokinase. Ginger is also mentioned in the page’s overview of its botanicals, but is not included in that six-item ingredient section. The listed other ingredients are purified water, vegetable glycerin, and natural flavoring. The supplied product image shows bottles and a 60-day badge, not a readable Supplement Facts panel. Ask the seller to confirm the complete current label, ingredient sources, and per-ingredient amounts before buying.

The vendor says the liquid is alcohol-free, made in the United States, and third-party tested. We found no linked test report, so these remain unverified vendor statements. Recurring-billing terms are not clear on the sales page; check checkout for subscriptions, renewals, or upsells.

Important distinction: a 600 mg proprietary blend total does not tell you how much boswellia, curcumin, nattokinase, or any other listed component is in a serving. The accessible bottle image is product photography, not the full ingredient label.

How LYMPH TONIC is intended to work

The vendor says the botanical blend is designed to support the body’s natural lymphatic drainage and circulation and may support healthy fluid balance. Those are marketing descriptions. The page does not show product-specific clinical research demonstrating that this exact formula reduces swelling, improves lymph flow, or treats lymphedema or water retention. No supplement should be used as a substitute for medical assessment of persistent or sudden swelling.

The listed ingredients are not interchangeable. Evidence on one botanical or nutrient cannot establish the effectiveness, safety, or appropriate dose of a multi-ingredient proprietary blend. This is especially relevant here because individual quantities are not provided.

What independent sources say about the key ingredients

Horse chestnut extract

The National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) summarizes a 2012 review of 17 studies suggesting standardized horse chestnut seed extract may improve symptoms of chronic venous insufficiency. NCCIH says more rigorous, larger trials are needed and notes possible side effects. That evidence is about studied standardized extract for a different condition, not LYMPH TONIC or its undisclosed dose.

Curcumin

NCCIH’s turmeric overview says evidence is insufficient for definitive conclusions about most proposed health uses. It notes that formulations differ and that some enhanced-bioavailability products have been associated with liver injury. Research on turmeric or curcumin does not demonstrate that this product works or establish the amount in its blend.

Omega-3 fatty acids

The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements explains in its consumer fact sheet that evidence differs by health outcome; for example, omega-3 supplements have not been shown to slow vision loss in people with age-related macular degeneration. High doses can interact with anticoagulants. The LYMPH TONIC sales page does not identify the omega-3 source or dose, so this broad nutrient evidence cannot verify the product’s claims.

Nattokinase

A 2017 review available through PubMed Central discusses early human and animal research on nattokinase, while also stating that important safety questions remain. It is not a clinical evaluation of LYMPH TONIC. Because the product does not disclose its nattokinase amount, people taking medicines that affect blood clotting should ask a clinician or pharmacist before considering it.
